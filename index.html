Early Stage — Mild Dementia

Memory issues are noticeable, but your loved one is largely independent. This stage can last several years. It's also the most important time to act — while they can still be part of the decisions that affect their life.

🧩 What You're Seeing
💭

Forgetting recent conversations, appointments, or names of familiar people

🗺️

Getting confused or lost in somewhat familiar places

💰

Difficulty with complex tasks: managing finances, planning events, solving problems

🐢

Taking much longer than usual to complete familiar tasks

😔

Withdrawing from social activities or hobbies they used to love

😤

Mood changes: frustration, anxiety, irritability, or sadness

💡 What to Know
❤️

They often know something is wrong. Many people in early stages feel frustrated, embarrassed, or frightened by what's happening. Patience and reassurance matter more than correct answers.

🗣️

This is the time for real conversations. While they can still participate meaningfully in decisions about their care, legal matters, and end-of-life wishes — have these conversations now, not later.

🌱

Routine and familiarity are your best tools. Consistent daily schedules reduce confusion and anxiety at every stage, but they're especially effective to establish now.

Middle Stage — Moderate Dementia

Often the longest and most demanding stage for caregivers. Your loved one needs significant help with daily activities and may show behavioral changes that are difficult to navigate. Consistency and routine become more critical than ever.

🧩 What You're Seeing
👨‍👩‍👧

May forget close family members' names or struggle to recognize faces

🛁

Needs help with daily activities: dressing, bathing, meals, and personal hygiene

🚶

Wandering — especially at night — a serious and urgent safety risk

🌙

"Sundowning" — increased confusion, agitation, or distress in the late afternoon and evening

🔄

Repetitive questions, statements, or actions throughout the day

😠

Agitation, paranoia, suspicion, or sudden unpredictable mood changes

🗣️

Increasing difficulty finding words, following conversations, or expressing needs

💡 What to Know
🔁

Routine is medicine. Consistent daily schedules significantly reduce agitation and confusion. Even small disruptions — a holiday, a visitor, a rearranged room — can cause big setbacks.

🔍

Every behavior is communication. Agitation, resistance, or repeated questions are almost always triggered by something: pain, hunger, fear, overstimulation, or an unmet need. The Behavior Log helps you find these patterns.

💪

Caregiver burnout is a medical emergency. You cannot sustain this without rest. Respite care isn't a luxury — it's how you survive this stage without destroying your own health.

Late Stage — Severe Dementia

The focus shifts from management to comfort and presence. Your loved one may have very limited communication and need full care for everything — but moments of connection, through music, touch, and familiar voices, are still possible and still matter.

🧩 What You're Seeing
🤫

Little or no verbal communication; may use sounds, gestures, or facial expressions

😶

May no longer recognize close family members by name or face

🛏️

Sleeping much of the time; significantly limited mobility

🍽️

Difficulty swallowing — needs modified food textures or thickened liquids

🦠

Highly vulnerable to infections, especially pneumonia and urinary tract infections

🩺

Completely dependent for all personal care: bathing, dressing, repositioning, toileting

💡 What to Know
🎵

Connection is still possible. Music from their past, familiar scents, a gentle hand on their arm, or the sound of your voice reading aloud — these can still reach them in meaningful ways, even when words can't.

💊

Pain may not be visible. In late stages, people often cannot express pain verbally. Watch for facial grimacing, restlessness, moaning, changes in breathing, or resistance to touch — these may all signal discomfort.

🕊️

Hospice is not giving up. Hospice is a shift in focus — toward comfort, dignity, and quality of the remaining time. It almost always means more support for your loved one and for your family. Ask their doctor about eligibility.

🔍 Not Sure What Stage You're In?

That's completely understandable — dementia stages aren't always clear-cut, and doctors don't always volunteer a stage label. Here's how to get oriented:

📋

Start with the Early Stage tab. Most families come to Lantern shortly after a diagnosis, which typically falls in the early-to-middle range. The Early Stage guidance is the most broadly applicable starting point.

🩺

Ask directly. "What stage would you say we're in, and what should we be prioritizing right now?" is a completely reasonable question. Doctors often won't volunteer this unless asked.

⚖️

The legal decisions are urgent regardless of stage. Power of Attorney and Advance Directives should be put in place as soon as possible after any dementia diagnosis, while your loved one can still participate.

💡 Routine Tips
⏰

Consistency is everything. The goal isn't a perfect schedule — it's predictability. Even an imperfect routine, followed consistently, dramatically reduces confusion and anxiety.

🌅

Anchor with familiar rituals. Morning coffee, an afternoon TV show, an evening hand massage — regular rituals signal safety and time of day far better than clocks can.

🖨️

Post it everywhere. Print this schedule and post it in the kitchen, bathroom, and bedroom. Give a copy to every person who provides care.

For each statement, mark how true it is right now. There are no right or wrong answers — this is a tool for honest reflection, not a judgment.

❤️ A Note on This Decision
💙

Placing a loved one in memory care is not abandonment. When care needs exceed what can be safely provided at home, a facility staffed with dementia-trained professionals can offer a quality of life that home care can no longer match.

📋

Research now, decide later. Visiting 2–3 facilities before a decision is needed dramatically reduces family stress. Good memory care facilities often have waiting lists of 3–6 months.

👨‍👩‍👧

The guilt is normal — and it doesn't mean you made the wrong choice. Nearly every caregiver who moves a loved one to memory care reports feeling guilty. That guilt is a measure of your love, not evidence that you failed.
